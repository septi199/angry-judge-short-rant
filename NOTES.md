# Notes — why this entry

## The brief

Write one short, funny rant (1–400 characters) about a small everyday
annoyance. A furious judge model compares rants two at a time and picks the
one that lands hardest. The judge weighs, in order:

1. A funny rant about an everyday annoyance (not an essay or a list).
2. Whether it is actually funny.
3. A specific, concrete annoyance, not a vague mood.
4. Sharp, economical wording — every sentence earning its place.
5. Freshness — an angle the judge has not heard a hundred times before.

## Why the self-checkout

- **Specific and concrete.** One machine, one accusation, one named phrase
  ("UNEXPECTED ITEM IN BAGGING AREA") that anyone who has used a
  self-checkout recognises instantly. The judge's criterion 3 is satisfied
  by the scene itself, not by adjectives.
- **Fresh angle.** The brief's own examples are a broken printer, a slow
  bus, a Monday morning — the most crowded angles in the field. The rant
  instead frames the machine as the aggressor and the shopper as the
  suspect: the scanner *saw* the scan and still raised the alarm. That
  inversion (accused of smuggling your own milk) is the joke, and it is
  not the version of this complaint the judge will have seen most.
- **Shared anger, not performed anger.** The fury is proportionate and
  recognisable — the bored teenager with the secret code, the empty staffed
  lane "laughing at me". No shouting in caps, no list of grievances; the
  situation does the shouting.
- **Economical.** Five short beats, each one sentence or a fragment:
  the accusation, the teenager, the tally ("Three items. Twenty minutes."),
  the punchline. Nothing to cut, no throat-clearing, well inside the
  400-character cap.

## Alternatives considered

- **Broken printer:** the brief's own example — the least fresh angle
  available, almost certain to collide with other entries.
- **Slow bus / Monday morning:** same problem; both are the first ideas
  every entrant has.
- **"Per my last email" / "circling back":** concrete and office-familiar,
  but the anger reads as workplace venting rather than a universally
  shared everyday annoyance, and the joke lands softer without a scene.

## Determinism

The entry is a fixed string in `main.ts`. The program writes it, reads it
back, and throws on any mismatch, so the graded file is exactly the text
above — no randomness, no model calls, no drift between runs.
